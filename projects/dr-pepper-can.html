Chapter 01 — Why

A scholarship, a soda can, and 20 days.

As part of a scholarship application I made a 43-inch tall, self-opening Dr. Pepper can.

Thanks to years I dedicated at a makerspace independently learning, rapidly prototyping and helping others start projects from scratch, I completed the project ideation, CAD, coding, wiring, fabrication, sourcing of materials and components, assembly and cosmetics — all within 20 days.

My initial idea was a fully refrigerated custom unit, but with 20 days on the line I had to make sure I had an MVP for my video — especially since you can't tell temperature from a video.

Chapter 02 — How it works

Press the tab. The can does the rest.

  1. Press the pull tabPressing the tab down clicks a limit switch, placed where a pushed-down tab would normally touch the lid.
  2. The servo opens the canA servo mounted under the lid, with two arms linked to the opening tab, slowly pulls it open.
  3. The tab follows alongAn elastic connector joins the pull tab to the opening tab, and the pull tab is hinged at the opening's edge — so it moves with it, just like a real can opening itself.
  4. The elevator risesOnce the servo reaches a set angle, an internal rack-and-pinion elevator driven by a DC motor lifts a can up and out of the opening.
  5. ResetAfter a few seconds it lowers until it hits a second limit switch — only then does the servo return to its starting position.

Chapter 04 — Planning

Measuring a real can, then modeling every part.

The first step was to take measurements from a real can and begin to model each part in SolidWorks — the lid and its mechanism, the shoulders, the laser-cut frame, and the brackets holding the motors.

Chapter 05 — Manufacturing

Print, putty, sand, paint. Repeat.

The complex geometry was 3D printed — namely the lid, shoulders and the bottom. To get rid of layer lines, red automotive putty was applied to the seams formed by gluing the printed parts together. The putty was then sanded down and sprayed with silver reflective spray paint.

The frame of the can was made out of ¼" plywood that I cut on a CO₂ laser, with rings and ribs designed to interlock with each other.

Chapter 06 — Electronics, code & video

Built to survive a power cut.

The code was written so the entire system could be powered off at any point — it resets itself as soon as the Arduino receives power again.

The outline of the video was written on a whiteboard the day of the shoot. The whole video was shot in one full day, and thanks to my friend Leo, editing took just one more.
